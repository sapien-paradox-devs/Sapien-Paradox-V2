Onboarding And Entitlement

One door a reader comes through, one question about whether they may read, and four identity cases decided in advance.

AcquisitionD25D26D50D64built

Why it exists

Two of the three seams live here. onboarding.create_reader(...) is the only way a reader comes into existence — the admin calls it, the payment legs call it, seed_dev calls it — so "how does a reader get here?" has one answer and payments arrived as a new caller rather than a new flow. access.can_read(user, chapter) is the only access check, which is why cadence could later be added by editing one function instead of auditing every query.

Identity is the hard part, and it is not decided inside the service. The acquisition machine resolves who the reader is and hands create_reader either a resolved user or None; the service's job is the transaction.

The transaction boundary

The four identity cases

D26 decided these in advance, and they are rows in the acquisition table rather than branches in a function. Rows are read top to bottom and the first whose guards pass fires, so the order is part of the logic.

#SituationOutcome
0This payment reference already has an orderStop. duplicate — the other leg got there first.
1Neither email nor phone is takenA new reader: user, order, grant, schedule.
2Known reader who already owns this bookAdopt the reference, then refuse already_owns_book. They want the links again, not a second charge.
3Known reader, new bookNormal. Order is unique per (user, book), not per user.
4aEmail names one reader, phone names anotherRefuse two_readers. Guessing here merges two people.
4bOne field matched, the other did notRefuse partial_match — kept distinct from 4a, because the frontend says something different.
Row order, concretely

Move the duplicate check off the top and a Razorpay retry creates a second reader. Move case 2 below case 3 and a returning reader is silently charged twice for the same book. Neither is a subtle bug in the table; both are invisible in a nest of ifs.

What each part does

01
core/machines/acquisition/machine.py
The table above, as data. Two states — new and fulfilled — and no refused state: a refusal is an outcome carried as a code, and the attempt is discarded either way.
02
core/machines/acquisition/guards.py
already_fulfilled, book_unknown, identity_unknown, is_exact_match, is_email_only_match, owns_book, matches_two_readers. They look things up through ctx.deps, so the machine's tests need no database.
03
core/api/checkout.py · acquisition_deps()
Where those lookups are bound to the ORM: find by email, find by phone, book by slug, order for a user and book, order by reference, adopt a reference, and create_reader itself.
04
core/services/onboarding.py · create_reader
The transaction in the figure, then delivery, then the result. It normalises the phone to one spelling, sets an unusable password on a new reader, refuses already_owns_book and book_has_no_chapters from inside the block, and mints a reset token only for someone who cannot yet log in.
05
core/services/onboarding.py · the admin's other actions
Update, deactivate, reactivate and erase, each leaving an AccountChange row. None of them deletes a reader — orders, grants and payment references are what refunds and accounting read. Covered in feature 17.
06
core/services/access.py · can_read
Two questions in one function: does this active reader own the book, and has this chapter unlocked. Returns a plain bool and never raises — a service raising an error whose only purpose is to become a 403 has put HTTP in the service layer.
07
core/services/access.py · owns
The first of the two questions, exposed on its own for exactly one caller: the cadence tick, whose chapter is locked by definition, so can_read would refuse the very grant it is unlocking.

Gotchas

  • "Has it unlocked?" is not "is unlock_at in the past?" A chapter is locked while the reader holds a scheduled grant for it, because the tick is the one clock. Comparing timestamps as well would make two clocks that disagree for up to fifteen minutes at every edge.
  • Ownership is re-checked on every request, including token-authenticated ones. So a refund or a deactivation takes effect on the reader's next request rather than whenever their token happens to expire.
  • is_active is checked inside the access question, not at login. That is what makes deactivating a reader stop the links already sitting in their WhatsApp history, and stop the tick sending them the rest of the book.
  • Never call create_reader inside an outer atomic(). The delivery that follows the block would be handed a link to a grant that is not committed yet.
  • ONBOARDING_ALLOW_PHONE_REUSE is a live hole behind a flag. It exists so the whole flow can be tested with one phone number, and it is a row in the table rather than a branch in a function precisely so that reverting it is deleting six lines. With it on, anyone who knows a reader's number can attach a purchase to their account. It must be unset and the row deleted before a real reader signs up.
  • A refund has nowhere to write. can_read asks whether an Order exists, and nothing revokes one — money can come in and cannot go back out. The fix is one nullable revoked_at and one clause in this function, which is the point of having a single access check.
  • Identity resolution left the service. It used to live in _resolve_identity inside onboarding.py, where the four cases were nested conditionals. Moving them into the table is why case 2 now adopts the payment reference on its way past — behaviour that was easy to forget and hard to see.
